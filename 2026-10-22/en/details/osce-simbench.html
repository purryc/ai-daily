Practice access is not clinical competence

Organizing standardized-patient practice requires people, scenarios and feedback, making repeated complete interviews difficult to schedule. The OSCE-SimBench research separates preparation from practice: author and review scenarios, let students question a simulated patient through text, then inspect checklist feedback after the encounter. Submitted at 07:08 UTC on October 21, it falls before the main window and is a three-day supplement. This is an educational and evaluation resource. Simulated responses are not guidance for treating real patients, and immediate scores are not professional certification. Its product value should concern access to useful practice and feedback quality rather than conversation volume alone.

Inspect scenarios and scoring separately

The pipeline organizes scenarios around symptoms and patient profiles. GPT-4.1 helps prepare checklists and narratives with expert review; GPT-4o handles live patient role-play and automated evaluation. Students select a scenario, inspect instructions and vital signs, and ask questions in a timed text conversation. Short responses aim to preserve the patient role instead of volunteering the entire case, while final checklist scoring examines what the student asked. The Apache-2.0 repository contains authoring, terminal simulation and grading code but explicitly excludes the web application. Readers can study components without assuming a clone recreates that service; model calls still require credentials and incur costs.

Do not merge different evaluations

The static comparison asks 17 senior medical students to rate four models and a single expert reference across three cases and 70 inputs, producing 7,480 rating actions. Fluent, relevant sentences do not guarantee consistency across an encounter, and not every metric difference is statistically significant. A separate resource contains 49 platform sessions, 27 scenarios and 1,763 question–answer turns from the same participants; it is not an independent learning-outcome experiment. The dialogue dataset uses CC BY-NC-SA, distinct from the code license. Automated grading was not independently validated here, and the dataset page was inaccessible during this check. Described resources should not all be assumed directly retrievable. The study concerns Korean-language medical education, so results do not establish interview quality in other languages.

Connect feedback to the next human practice

Human standardized patients, instructor review and peer role-play remain alternatives, especially for prosody, facial expression and gesture. The text prototype cannot reproduce those cues, its expert reference comes from one person, and three evaluated cases cannot represent all specialties. A falsifiable pilot should carry omissions found in simulation into later human practice, checking follow-up quality, belief in fabricated details and instructor time correcting automated scores. Users should be able to inspect the turn behind a deduction and distinguish model scoring from instructor confirmation; these are our recommendations. More practice that raises automated scores without improving real communication would not establish educational value. Instructors should also be able to flag invalid scenarios, preventing one faulty fictional case from repeatedly reinforcing the same misconception across later practice sessions.
