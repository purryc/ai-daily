From recognizing actions to connecting responses

A teacher circles a word, a learner types an answer, and the teacher changes the hint. Recognizing circling and typing alone may not reveal which intervention addresses which difficulty. VIRA-Bench separates this local relationship into observable evidence grounding, interaction-contingency reasoning and support-response modeling. Submitted at 08:52 UTC on October 21, it qualifies for the main window. Its users are researchers developing multimodal tutoring analysis and dialogue systems who need to locate a missing link in interaction understanding. It is not designed to grade an individual teacher or pupil, and calling it an automated teaching product would exceed the evidence.

Questions need traceable clip evidence

The study extracts 15–60-second excerpts from online English tutoring, combining a shared screen with a learner-camera view. Visual changes locate interventions such as circling or dragging. Models draft structured descriptions, researchers correct them against the source, and generated questions are filtered for shortcuts that permit answering without the video. The result contains 1,005 clips and 1,322 questions, with additional expert sample audits. Construction and leaderboard models are separated to reduce overlap, without guaranteeing artifact-free annotations. Product teams can borrow the evidence-linking process: an analytical claim should point to a time, action and response instead of summarizing an entire lesson without inspectable support.

Preserve denominators and task boundaries

The best of 11 models scores 61.3% overall, against a 91.0% human reference. However, people answer a stratified 200-question subset while models answer all 1,322, so the difference is not a precise same-denominator estimate for the complete benchmark. Removing video most affects evidence grounding; shuffling aligned video–transcript segments most affects interaction-contingency reasoning. Those interventions distinguish behavioral dependencies without identifying an internal model mechanism. The multiple-choice protocol does not evaluate open-ended generation, action or adaptation over multiple turns. Better recognition of classroom events therefore cannot automatically become a claim that a system can adjust its teaching strategy appropriately.

Keep uncertainty visible in tutoring analysis

The scope is short, one-to-one English tutoring, largely involving Mandarin-speaking learners. Results do not automatically transfer to groups, other subjects or long-term teaching. The paper is CC BY, while processed videos and transcripts involving minors have separate restrictions; the paper license cannot be applied to all data. Alternatives are instructor review, transcript search and simple screen-event markers. A falsifiable pilot should include easily confused event orders, require the system to acknowledge insufficient evidence, allow instructor correction, and record misattributions and review time. A polished summary that credits a later hint for an earlier answer increases confidence without making the analysis dependable. Acceptance should also separate missing transcript content from insufficient visual evidence, helping reviewers identify which source needs inspection before they trust the proposed interpretation.
