Preserve the contact skill, rearrange the coordination

When two arms stack bowls or pour nuts, a failure can happen between otherwise valid skills: one arm reaches for the bowl while the other is still pouring into it. ScheduleGen starts with a small set of annotated demonstrations. Grasping, pouring and releasing become skills expressed relative to objects, with explicit ordering or synchronization requirements. After objects move, the system adapts the end-effector poses and plans connections between skills. This reduces the need to demonstrate an entire sequence for every crossed layout, while leaving researchers responsible for usable skills and correct task relationships. Collisions determine which arm waits

Each arm first receives a candidate path. A joint scheduler then decides when its waypoints execute. A potential collision creates an either-or constraint: one arm clears the region before the other enters. Task precedence and synchronization remain binding. GPU collision checks use spherical geometry, including objects temporarily attached to a gripper. A mixed-integer linear program minimizes the overall duration and can incorporate preferences such as shorter pauses. Scheduled trajectories are replayed in parallel simulations; successful completions become training examples. The intervention therefore changes coordination timing instead of simply playing two independent demonstrations at once. Finger actions inside adapted skills remain those supplied by the source demonstration.

Generated demonstrations and learned performance differ

The default experiment uses five annotated source demonstrations per task and collects one thousand successful replays per variant before training a visuomotor policy. Across 28 simulated variants, generation success averaged 79.6%, while learned policies averaged 65.5%; these measure different stages. Transfer to seven externally authored RoboDojo tasks exposes a further boundary: policy success averaged 47.9% on nominal held-out layouts and 25.0% when object-pose perturbations doubled. On real YAM hardware, policies receive three RGB views plus joint and gripper states, and output joint-position and gripper targets. Eight physical variants each receive twenty trials, producing 50–80% success. Some use simulation-only sources; others begin with calibrated physical demonstrations.

Calibration and deployment checks remain

This method fits teams that already have task definitions, calibration, object geometry and seed skills. Ambiguous automated annotations still require human review, and source segments must pass replay and task-success checks. Timing constraints prevent waypoint speeds from increasing but do not explicitly constrain acceleration. Geometric approximations and simulation models can also miss physical contact conditions. The experiments demonstrate data generation and policy learning in specified settings; they do not establish that a learned policy inherits every collision constraint used by the planner. Deployment still requires checking failure states, changed layouts and contact behavior. The CC BY 4.0 paper reports author experiments, without independent reproduction here.
