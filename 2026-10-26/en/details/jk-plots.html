Small samples can look deceptively settled

A pie chart based on ten observations can still present sharply defined proportions, making a sample look more settled than it is. jk-Plots accepts univariate observations, retains their visible representation and adds distributions that could plausibly have produced them. Categorical data can use layered pie charts; continuous data can use familiar cumulative-distribution or boxplot forms. With little evidence, plausible models differ widely and graphical boundaries spread out. More observations can narrow that variation. No AI system is guessing a confidence score here. The visual uncertainty comes from statistical modeling, letting the graphic distinguish the observations from the range of population interpretations they support.

Fit the model before choosing its appearance

Implementation separates inference from display. Continuous values default to a Normal distribution and categorical values to a categorical model; the model estimates an intercept without predictors. The R prototype calls Stan through brms, using four chains that yield 16,000 posterior draws after warm-up. Each draw defines a plausible model, but displaying every one would be cluttered. Three summaries are implemented: select a subset, aggregate into interval bands, or fade around the central trend. Analysts still need appropriate distributional assumptions and priors. Replacing a chart function does not complete the inference. The paper discusses data-scaled priors and problems when extremely few observations leave a model underspecified.

Skewed data exposes the default model

The demonstrations use samples of ten, one hundred and one thousand from categorical, Student and Gamma distributions. In the categorical example, a small empirical pie chart gives precise observed shares while the plausible models still vary substantially. With more observations the two representations converge. The important counterexample uses a skewed, bounded Gamma distribution: the default Normal model is symmetric and unbounded, so a gradient boxplot cannot preserve the actual shape faithfully. An ordinary empirical boxplot is closer to the sample in that case. Blurring communicates a range of estimates but does not check whether the model is suitable. Keeping raw observations visible provides a useful comparison rather than a guarantee.

Expert interest is not a comprehension result

Interviews involved a statistician, two visualization researchers and an applied data scientist, with additional workplace feedback from one coauthor. That collaborator should not be counted as an independent external participant. Sessions lasted approximately 30–60 minutes, first discussing small-sample tasks and then prepared figures and the technical workflow. Experts saw potential for communicating uncertainty but questioned whether less statistically experienced audiences would understand the model parameters. Suggestions included descriptive overlays and a clearer central estimate. There was no controlled comprehension experiment, decision-accuracy measure or confidence-calibration test. This is a visualization prototype for analysts to investigate; its effect on a particular audience still needs evaluation before claiming that it prevents mistaken small-sample conclusions.
