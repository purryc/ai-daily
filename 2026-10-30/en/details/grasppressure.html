An input that fits around an occupied hand

Holding a plate or a tool makes reaching for a touchscreen disruptive, while moving fingers can disturb the object. GraspPressure studies a different action: keep the grasp and change the force applied by one finger to a contact surface. Its objects include a bottle, basket, pestle, small box, plate and marker, representing six grasp types. The questions concern more than detecting a squeeze: which fingers are comfortable, how much usable force remains, and whether people can select accurately when continuous pressure is divided into levels. This is a study of input capability with illustrative prototypes. It does not announce a purchasable universal controller or establish that every concurrent object-handling task can be performed successfully.

Measure and calibrate the person's pressure

The apparatus uses an Interlink FSR400 force-sensitive resistor, 5.08 millimeters across, with an amplifier circuit and Arduino Uno acquisition feeding a computer. Known weights calibrate its nonlinear response into force. The first study involved twelve people and compared six object grasps with an empty-hand baseline. Sensors were placed where each naturally positioned finger could press; uncomfortable fingers were excluded. The thumb was generally comfortable and strong, but that does not make it the fixed choice for every grasp. The selection experiment used the thumb with an upper force bound measured for each participant. Reported performance therefore depends on placement and calibration and cannot be translated directly into one universal threshold for a manufactured device.

More levels make selection harder

In the second twelve-person study, pressure moved an on-screen cursor. Participants reached a target, briefly stabilized and quickly released. The experiment crossed eight, ten and twelve levels with five target forces, producing 4,320 trials. Overall success was 92.7% and mean selection time was 1.74 seconds. Eight-level selection reached 94.48% success, versus 91.31% with twelve levels; corresponding mean times increased from 1.59 to 1.93 seconds. Object type did not show an equally consistent effect. Finer levels make small force changes cross boundaries, increasing adjustment. This supports considering modest numbers of controls, but does not establish equal performance without visual feedback, during walking or during another demanding task. Comparisons with older papers are not direct competitive conditions in this experiment.

The camera demonstrations need their own validation

Separate prototypes use PressureVision++ to estimate fingertip pressure from RGB images. They demonstrate changing television volume while holding a mug, stepping through a recipe with a pestle and confirming while holding a card. These illustrate a possible route without instrumenting every object, but do not validate everyday accuracy. In particular, the 92.7% FSR result cannot be assigned to camera sensing. A card confirmation gesture is also not identity authentication or payment security. A deployed interface would still need to handle occlusion, ordinary grip changes, varied hands and fatigue, while distinguishing stabilization force from intentional commands. Long-term false activation is not established. Keeping another control route is a design inference, not a tested finding from these prototypes.
