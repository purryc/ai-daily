A landing tutor adjusts shared control and feedback using recent practice

Practice first, then receive concrete feedback

The prototype places instruction inside a two-dimensional quadrotor landing simulator. Learners use a throttle for thrust and a joystick for roll angular acceleration, aiming to reach a pad within speed and attitude limits. After each attempt, the interface reports landing time, final speed and roll angle, landing category and total score. Learners then report confidence and mental workload. GPT-4o produces positive, neutral or negative formative feedback. The language model supplies that feedback text; a separate controller provides flight assistance. Describing the system as a chatbot flying for the user would therefore be misleading. The interaction connects concrete performance feedback with subsequent instructional choices instead of providing only a generic encouraging message.

A policy chooses assistance and feedback tone

The policy controls two things: whether shared control is enabled and which feedback tone follows an attempt. Shared control supplements the learner's input with a linear-quadratic regulator following a reference trajectory from expert demonstrations; manual mode leaves control to the learner. A learner model represents confidence, workload and learning stage. The new algorithm uses reports and scores from the recent ten trials to change the relative weights of three teaching objectives, then recomputes a policy through model-based planning. It is inspired by actor-critic methods, rather than allowing unrestricted online reinforcement-learning exploration. In the experiment, weights change at phase boundaries. Within a phase, the resulting policy chooses assistance and feedback for individual attempts.

Forty people train before an unaided evaluation

Forty participants began with two manual attempts without formative feedback, completed three training phases of ten trials each, and finished with five manual attempts without feedback. Starting positions changed between phases, with their order randomized to reduce ordering effects. The final five-trial average measured final performance, and its difference from the first two attempts measured change. Reports and scores informed the weights; subsequent clustering identified people whose priorities emphasized confidence, workload or skill progression, plus people whose priority category changed. These groups were derived from observed data rather than randomly assigned teaching conditions. Comparisons among them consequently do not isolate the effectiveness of a particular policy or establish a causal advantage for personalization.

[S134]

Changing priorities does not establish learning benefit

Sixteen participants changed priority category; the stable confidence, workload and skill-progression groups contained nine, eight and seven people. Final scores were higher for the workload group than the skill-progression group, but improvement from baseline did not differ significantly across groups. This cannot be rewritten as all adaptive users learning faster. The authors explicitly acknowledge that the study cannot determine the benefit of an updated policy compared with a static one. The prototype shows how self-reports and behavior can inform one instructional loop, while remaining dependent on subjective reports, a learning-stage classifier and predefined scoring rules. Short desktop sessions establish neither transfer to real flight nor long-term retention; a direct static-versus-adaptive comparison remains necessary.
