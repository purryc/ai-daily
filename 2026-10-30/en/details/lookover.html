Meeting a comment quota can leave discussion fragmented

A course can require annotations beside a reading and still leave each student talking largely alone. The authors analyzed six weeks of Perusall activity from a graduate course: 55 students, 18 readings and 2,873 comments, of which 53.5% received no reply. The assignment required at least three constructive comments per reading, with much activity concentrated near deadlines. These are observations of the existing course, not outcomes from Lookover. They expose a specific entry problem: a student needs to locate an active discussion and decide where a contribution fits. Asking for more comments does not provide that orientation. Lookover consequently links two operations, finding a discussion and developing a response, in the reading interface.

A theme must lead back to a peer's actual comment

Built on the open-source Hypothesis annotation platform, Lookover appears as a sidebar add-on. Discussion Overview groups a document's comments into a small set of themes, each with a title and a brief account of the arguments. Opening a theme exposes a fuller summary and the comments behind it, with their passage anchors and reply links. A student can begin with an issue and enter a particular exchange instead of searching the whole reading passage by passage. The theme is not a replacement summary detached from the text: the original peer contribution remains the object of a reply. The paper does not establish commercial pricing or large-scale deployment performance; its concrete contribution is making dispersed discussion navigable.

The editor supplies questions while students supply arguments

Spark Thinking generates questions from the current passage and nearby comments when a student opens the editor. Understand questions clarify what the text or a peer is claiming. Challenge questions address the reasoning. Connect questions point toward relationships elsewhere in the reading. A link to specific peer comments gives the student something concrete to answer. The system does not place a finished answer into the comment box or compose the student's final contribution. It removes part of the work of finding an entry point while leaving reading, judgment and expression to the student. A question alone need not produce a deeper answer: when background knowledge is limited, even a useful prompt can still lead to a short reaction.

A five-person trial exposes discovery and knowledge gaps

The preliminary evaluation gave five participants a real reading and no introduction to the features. People who discovered the overview valued it, but one participant who read by linear scrolling never found it. Questions also worked differently: a knowledgeable participant found new angles, whereas an unfamiliar participant who rated the feature highly and used it repeatedly still wrote brief reactions. Liking a feature therefore cannot be equated with learning gains. The authors plan a classroom deployment with an AI-free comprehension test, and instructor interviews about alternatives to fixed comment counts. The interface can reduce the friction of finding an exchange; sustaining discussion after submission also depends on assignment design, not simply on adding another control.
