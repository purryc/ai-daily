Start with an opening the participant can continue

As-If Machine is a co-writing research tool. Participants provide background about everyday life, see a personalized opening describing how a hypothetical event begins, and continue the story in the first person. The system does not produce a complete finished essay at once. It offers short continuations when the person pauses; Tab accepts one, while rejection, revision and independent writing remain possible. This addresses the difficulty of beginning from an abstract scenario and makes each accepted suggestion an observable action. The paper uses the interface to study how imagined experience affects perceived risk. Its stories are experimental situations, not events the person actually experienced or reliable predictions of what will happen in the future.

Separate the opening, plot and next sentence

Four specialized roles divide the work. An Initializer writes the opening, a Predictor proposes consequences for the character, a Storyteller organizes a plot, and an Autocompleter uses that context and the current text to suggest a continuation. Researchers configure scenarios, intake questions, models and instructions through YAML. In the described current implementation, completion waits for a 300-millisecond pause and conditions such as an already completed sentence. Early informal testing found that people disliked the system assigning them internal states, leading the authors to constrain the completer from supplying their thoughts or feelings. This is an instruction governing generation, not a guarantee that every output complies or evidence that the system knows the participant's emotions.

Two controls separate the story from the writing format

The authors ran two preregistered randomized experiments with final samples of 1,007 for science funding and 894 for satellite cybersecurity. Each compared co-writing with two conditions: reading an expert vignette on the same topic and using the same interface for an unrelated scenario. Perceived closeness of the risk to one's life exceeded the vignette condition in both studies, by 0.36 and 0.38 standard deviations. Policy attitudes did not follow an equally uniform pattern: the science-funding contrast against unrelated writing was not significant, whereas satellite-related effects were larger. Making a story concrete and increasing agreement with a policy are separately measured outcomes. These results describe the chosen experiments, not arbitrary topics or populations.

Sentence control does not remove the scenario's influence

The deployment disclosed the general scenario before writing, and participants actively chose continuations, but researchers still controlled the event and agent instructions. The authors acknowledge that the system can make real or fabricated risks feel personally relevant; participation does not remove the possibility of influence. Outcomes were self-reported immediately after the task, so lasting attitudes and real behavior are unestablished. Personalization, the starting scenario and co-writing were evaluated as one intervention rather than isolated components. Participants came from Prolific, and both scenarios described negative futures. The findings therefore do not cover all writers or scenarios. For understanding the interface, sentence-level choice and control over the entire narrative are distinct; an accept button provides only the former.
