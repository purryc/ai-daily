Start with the object you want

For a small desk device, the first obstacle often arrives before any code: drawing an enclosure, choosing connectors and deciding whether the parts will fit. Confetti’s founders describe encountering exactly that friction while building a home AI device. Their proposed starting point is a conversation, with text or image input leading to a design that the maker can continue changing. The practical question is how easily a person can revise the object after noticing a problem. A model that looks convincing on screen is still only one stage of a physical project; its appearance alone does not establish that the assembled device will work.

Keep component choices beside the design

The electronics side covers off-the-shelf component sourcing plus help with wiring and firmware. That addresses a familiar handoff: a mechanical idea becomes an electronics shopping list, which then needs software to do anything useful. Keeping those choices in one project could reduce repeated transcription of dimensions and requirements. It does not establish universal compatibility. A connector name, for example, is not a complete electrical specification, and an enclosure must leave room for access as well as the component itself. These are engineering implications of the proposed workflow. The public launch material does not describe a comprehensive validation system or identify the model used to generate firmware.

A print quote is not an assembled device

Manufacturing has a concrete handoff: obtain a quote and send supported 3D printing jobs to print farms, which deliver the printed parts. That is more useful than a design tool ending at a file export, but the deliverable matters. A printed enclosure is different from an assembled, wired and tested electronic product. Sharing and remixing offer another way to begin, particularly when the desired change concerns an existing shape. The founders mention laboratory equipment, keyboards and desk companions as examples of ongoing work. Those examples show the range they are pursuing; they are not independent evidence that every type of device has been completed or manufactured reliably.

Make the first prototype inspectable

The October 29 Product Hunt board supplies a dated discovery point, not a verified first availability or shipment date. A sensible first project would produce inspectable outputs: a model, identifiable parts and a printed object whose fit can be checked. Complexity should follow the object’s requirements rather than the number of automated steps available in a chat. Manufacturing regions, supported materials, tolerances and return terms are not fully established by the launch material reviewed here. For the prospective maker, the important outcome is a design clear enough that another person or manufacturer can build the same object, with unresolved decisions visible before money and materials are committed.
