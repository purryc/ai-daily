Keep meaningful decisions with the author

CoWriter is a research platform for long, source-grounded writing, combining chat, a shared document canvas, and a source explorer. Its leader agent does more than await edits: it invites participation during goal definition, brainstorming, outlining, drafting, revision, copyediting, and final review. The invitation changes with the activity, from clarifying audience to comparing an outline or contributing a personal example. Stages can be revisited, and ad hoc requests can interrupt the process. The product direction is continuous content choice instead of a single approval decision after the machine has produced everything. The evaluated task was a 750–1,000-word source-based blog post, so the results should not be generalized automatically to fiction, correspondence, or every form of professional authorship.

Three roles work around one document

The architecture assigns responsibilities to a Leader, Monitor, and Reviewer. The Monitor observes messages and document changes, maintains a Writing Log containing goals, visited stages, and an event timeline, and reminds the leader about unfinished requests and possible next steps. The Reviewer reads the current document and goal without the earlier chat or activity history, then returns a reader-oriented critique. Yjs coordinates concurrent document editing; renaming an entity can prompt the agent to update other mentions. This reduces repeated instructions but also means a local human edit may cause broader changes. Message-level diff views help expose those changes. The separate reviewer is a design choice inside the same AI system, not an independent human fact-checking service.

More participation, smaller units of work

Fourteen graduate students with academic writing experience completed both conditions with counterbalanced system order and topics. The systems shared their model and interface environment. CoWriter produced 235 successful engagement instances versus 109 for the baseline, with 0.57 invitations per hundred agent words versus 0.24 and response ratios of 57% versus 47%. Mean sessions lasted 62 versus 68 minutes. There were more agent messages, 25 versus 16, but 27% fewer agent words overall and smaller document changes. The 2.2-fold figure concerns invitations that elicited responses, not doubled writing quality. Shorter exchanges may support participation, but this small, supplied-source study does not establish daily retention, willingness to pay, or gains for writers without academic experience.

Citation granularity and authorship belong together

Source support is more specific than attaching a bibliography. CoWriter links claims to smaller source spans: the paper reports averages of 205 tokens versus 12,754 for the baseline, while automatic citation F1 was 0.84 for both. Authors reported stronger engagement and ownership, and external readers favored personal-experience and storytelling qualities. However, the baseline removed the monitor, reviewer, log, and localized attribution together, preventing attribution of the benefit to one role alone. Code and data were promised on publication, with no verified consumer price. My editorial judgment is that collaboration should be paced around meaningful human contributions. Smaller citations and smaller edits matter for the same reason: they make the material manageable enough for a person to read and decide.
