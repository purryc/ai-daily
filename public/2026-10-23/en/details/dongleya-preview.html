Start with something to explore together

Dongleya calls its interactive creations “wonder cards.” An original October 19 report describes a limited Tencent preview for families and teachers, accessible through a website and a WeChat mini program. October 21 coverage follows that report rather than establishing another launch. The community supplies a starting library, so a parent does not need to begin by writing a prompt. Later coverage describes categories by education stage and subjects including language, mathematics, science and art. Our interpretation is that choosing a card around an existing question offers a more approachable beginning than an empty conversation box. A family can start by exploring material together and only then decide whether it wants to change or create something.

From a Gomoku request to a playable board

The clearest external experience is the original reporter’s Gomoku test. After a request in the chat box, a playable game appeared in the right-hand pane, with download, community publication and WeChat or QQ sharing options. This demonstrates one working path from language to an interactive result, without proving that every complex lesson generates equally well. A possible family activity, in our view, is for the child to choose a theme and rules while an adult helps express the request. They can try the first version and discuss changes together. A familiar-object sorting game would let the child help design the questions and interaction. We have not signed in or repeated the reporter’s test.

Remixing avoids a blank starting point

The public usage guide separates browsing, creation, editing and sharing, and describes text, voice and recorded-material input. An inspiration wall offers prepared topics; existing community work can also be remixed. For a teacher, the potential value is retaining an interaction while adapting its characters, wording or exercise content to a particular lesson. A parent could continue a theme the child already enjoys. Those are uses inferred from the product mechanics, not measured learning gains. Interactive material has state and rules, so changing one detail can alter how an activity behaves. The format consequently invites non-programmers into the making of small interactive experiences, with more to revise than the illustration or wording of a static explanation.

Promising for shared exploration, with limited access

What is established is a reported limited preview, not guaranteed admission for every account. The official entry was inaccessible in this research environment, and long-term prices, quotas, moderation and family-account arrangements remain unverified. The original coverage also raises factual rigor and loading demands. Our judgment is that an activity is most useful as an object of conversation: a child explains what happened, an adult asks why, and they decide whether anything should change. Movement on a screen should not substitute for an explanation. The explore–revise–share sequence could support making things together. Whether families return depends on the activities themselves; the available reporting supplies neither retention evidence nor measured educational outcomes.
