Task and evidence

The central issue in Saviynt’s announcement is that permission for individual actions does not make their combination appropriate. A request to pay an existing vendor could become a sequence that first creates a new vendor and then pays it. Each action might be permitted while the combined business intent has changed. Zuma for Apps proposes connecting the initiating person, orchestrator, subagents, tools, and final application transactions, then allowing, blocking, or escalating before execution. Mechanism and limits

The established workflow combines identity permissions, application-specific approvals, and manual review of high-risk transactions. Each layer may see only its own account and action. The new product hypothesis is that governance can evaluate intent and separation-of-duties constraints across the chain, then enforce that decision at the execution boundary. SAP Joule skills reached through Saviynt’s MCP server are the concrete vendor example. Security teams want to prevent apparently legitimate combinations from violating policy. Application owners also need to explain why a necessary transaction was blocked and how an exception can proceed with accountable review.

Control and recovery

The decisive limit is whether the gateway actually controls the transaction. Mapping relationships or finding potential exposure is different from observing an executed transaction, and demonstration monetary figures should not be presented as customer losses. An agent, legacy script, or person might still reach an application through a direct path outside enforcement. A comprehensive-looking policy diagram could therefore cover only part of real activity. Actual policy enforcement, bypass attempts, false-positive rates, and rollback were not independently tested. Pre-execution governance should be described as the vendor’s proposed control, not as an established security outcome.

Adoption and costs

Interaction design needs to support both ordinary work and recovery. A user should be able to see who must approve a pending action, which rule caused a denial, what can be changed, and whether that change triggers reevaluation of the whole chain. The supplied evidence does not resolve recovery when one application has already executed its part and another is blocked. Connector coverage, legacy interfaces, conflicting policies, and reviewer workload are substantial adoption questions. This private preview is distinct from the existing Zuma free trial. Pricing and general availability remain unknown, preventing preview capabilities from becoming a reliable delivery commitment.

[S27]

Acceptance and alternatives

A falsifiable acceptance exercise would compare two nearly identical flows: paying an existing vendor and creating a vendor before paying it. The system should preserve the legitimate flow while explaining and blocking the added action where policy requires. Reviewers should then verify that a denied operation cannot simply complete through a direct alternative path, and reconstruct the reasoning behind an approved exception. False positives and review time should be compared with native application approvals and manual high-risk review. More relationship diagrams without dependable enforcement, or an exception queue that overwhelms reviewers, would not establish value. Coverage and recovery determine the purchasing case.
