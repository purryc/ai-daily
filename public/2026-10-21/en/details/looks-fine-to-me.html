“Looks Fine to Me” exposes the oversight funnel: checking is not the same as detecting silent defects

Evidence and scope

The most difficult agent output to supervise is often not an obvious error but something plausible that misses one requirement. “Looks Fine to Me” uses a formative study with 12 people and an experiment with 36 participants to examine correct outputs, silent defects and visible failures, drawing on interaction logs, gaze and physiological recordings. The important product distinction is to break “the user checked it” into stages: initiating further evaluation, choosing a task-relevant check, exposing the defect, recognizing it and finally resolving it. A confirmation button or a longer activity trace does not automatically carry the reviewer through those stages. Each transition can fail even when the user is present and apparently active.

Evidence and scope

Among 71 silent-defect instances, 65 received additional evaluation, 40 received task-relevant evaluation, 35 had evaluation that revealed the issue, 30 were recognized and 26 were resolved through the main path, with two additional manual repairs. These counts belong to one funnel and should retain that instance-based interpretation. Changing denominators opportunistically can obscure the loss between stages, while reporting that a test occurred can misleadingly imply that the defect was found. Participants sometimes checked the next task more closely after a visible failure, but observations within limited task sequences do not establish that one failure produces sustained vigilance in general. The study also did not produce a reliable generic engagement detector. Gaze or physiological measures should not be converted into a claimed capability to determine which workers are paying adequate attention.

Acceptance and alternatives

An actionable design connects evidence to requirements rather than displaying only the amount of agent activity. If a requirement concerns a saved field, independently read the final record. If it concerns a boundary case, present a relevant example instead of asking the same generation process to endorse itself. Manual inspection, independent tests, sampling and a second reviewer remain meaningful alternatives.

Acceptance and alternatives

Recovery states should distinguish unchecked requirements, checks that did not establish correctness, and discovered defects awaiting repair; one green status can hide all three. A falsifiable judgment compares genuine recognition and resolution under the same tasks and defect distribution, while checking whether reviewers understand which requirements still lack evidence. More clicks, longer dwell time or greater confidence would not satisfy that judgment if silent defects remain unresolved. The study’s practical contribution is a way to locate failure in oversight, not a universal prescription for how long a person should look at an output. Its limited sample and task setting also constrain extrapolation to prolonged professional use.
