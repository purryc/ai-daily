Documents become editable argument objects

GroundWork takes a document collection and represents claims, evidence, assumptions and objections as editable argument components linked to passages. For a product analyst, the useful starting task would be a disputed conclusion, not a request to summarize everything. Ask which source actually supports the conclusion and which condition could make that support fail. This is our proposed use, not an evaluated deployment. A graph gives the analyst something specific to inspect, but neat placement can also conceal uncertainty. The reader still has to decide whether the selected passage is relevant to the particular question being asked.

Suggestions remain separate from commitments

Model proposals are reviewed before promotion into the working argument. That distinction is useful when brainstorming objections: generating an objection should not silently convert it into an accepted fact. In a team review, we would want an adoption decision and its reason to stay visible, so another reader could distinguish a temporary possibility from a commitment. Those are implementation criteria for a later deployment, rather than demonstrated collaboration benefits here. A good trial should include deliberately misleading evidence and measure whether participants notice the weakness instead of merely producing a larger, more polished map.

[S31]

Compare disagreement against the same evidence

An evidence-by-claim comparison helps expose differing assumptions and lets the user revise model-assigned relationships. A practical evaluation would replace one weak source and observe whether the analyst revisits the conclusions that depended on it. Counting nodes would miss this behavior. Multiple articles can repeat the same original claim, so apparent breadth is not independent corroboration. For everyday work, the map is worthwhile only if returning to the original passage is easier than reopening several documents and reconstructing the reasoning manually. That comparison has not been established by the reported study.

[S31]

The study examines fit rather than efficiency

The evidence consists of four expert walkthroughs and three use cases, with limitations around evolving corpora, tables and conditional reasoning. It does not establish comparative or long-term performance. For a short document, building a map could cost more effort than direct reading; for a long project, maintaining it becomes work of its own. An adoption decision should therefore examine one real revision: whether qualifications survive, whether mistaken support can be located, and whether upkeep is manageable. Commercial access and pricing were not established. The paper offers a concrete interaction design to test, not a measured promise of better professional decisions.
