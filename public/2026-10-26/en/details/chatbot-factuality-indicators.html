Change the cue beneath the answer

The paper examines a concrete design decision: what should appear beneath a chatbot answer to communicate its reliability? Eight interviews and a 1,000-person survey informed six mobile interface conditions: no cue, high/low confidence text, a percentage, a prominent confidence bubble, source icons, and sources combined with confidence language. The experimental percentages were 85 and 30, deliberately selected stimuli rather than calibrated estimates from a running model. Readers encountered an answer and a cue, then judged the system. This is evidence about presenting reliability information, not the launch of a chatbot capable of independently verifying every statement or a validated method for calculating those percentages.

First impressions before and after ground truth

The experiment involved 5,151 US adults who used generative AI at least monthly, recruited through YouGov’s online panel. Each participant was randomly assigned one topic, cue type and correct or incorrect response. Topics covered gardening, mathematics and medicine. They viewed a smartphone screenshot for at least 30 seconds before rating accuracy, information sufficiency, trust and sentiment. A subsequent screen explicitly revealed correctness, after which trust and impression were rated again. Data collection occurred in June 2025; the new announcement concerns research disclosure, not a study conducted this week. This separates initial impression from informed reaction, while omitting follow-up dialogue, source navigation and consequential use of advice.

A measurable but small first-impression effect

High-confidence responses were perceived as more accurate, and some confidence or source labels received more favorable initial trust or impressions. The paper stresses that indicator-type effects were small; many comparisons did not differ significantly from showing no indicator at all. Once correctness was revealed, display-type differences in trust and sentiment disappeared and actual correctness had a much larger influence. A confidently presented error could produce a stronger trust decline. Medical topics elicited greater skepticism overall, so the gardening result cannot simply be transferred to higher-stakes use. These findings neither select one universally superior icon nor establish that displaying a percentage improves people’s practical ability to detect errors.

Sources still have to be usable

Interviewees interpreted confidence variously as probability of correctness, agreement among sources or evidence that checking had already happened. Those meanings imply different guarantees, making an unexplained label easy to misread. Participants wanted relevant source passages, expandable evidence and indications of uncertainty in particular answer spans. The authors propose these directions; the experiment does not validate them as improvements. All samples were US-based regular AI users, interviewees were relatively young, and the experiment supplied only one static screenshot without a behavioral decision. The useful product question is how to make evidence interpretable and inspectable. Reducing verification steps merits testing, but this study does not quantify added source clicks or reduced misuse.
