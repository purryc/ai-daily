Start with the encounter you want to practice

GELLI addresses a specific preparation problem: a learner may want to rehearse an encounter for which no suitable script exists. Its setup separates setting, task, grammar and vocabulary fields. Any subset can be supplied, with the system suggesting the rest. Once a goal exists, the learner previews the role and situation and can regenerate them. A narrative module groups goals that fit one social encounter, then creates a character and setting. Responses follow what the learner actually says rather than a fixed sequence of dialogue choices. This removes some lesson-authoring work while leaving difficulty selection unresolved: participants sometimes supplied an ambiguous setting or invented an encounter beyond their language ability.

Help beside the character without supplying the whole answer

The scene uses foreground character art, a background and narration displayed at room scale rather than a continuously rendered three-dimensional world. Push-to-talk speech is transcribed into a message log. Dialogue generation reads that log, the scene and its goals, then produces the character’s reply and English narration. Adjacent panels offer target tracking, Japanese replay, translations and furigana. Suggestions contain words or phrases instead of complete answers. When an utterance fails in context, the character expresses confusion while a separate panel explains a correction. Reflection, transcript and goals inform feedback before context passes to the next scene. The SwiftUI implementation runs on Vision Pro with separate OpenAI text, image, speech and transcription models.

Matched goals on the same headset

The study recruited 47 people; exclusions left 40 for quantitative comparison and 43 for qualitative analysis. Participants had at least a year of university Japanese or equivalent experience. Each tried generated practice and expert-authored videos, with café versus shopping contexts and presentation order counterbalanced. Both used the same headset; video participants also shadowed speech and paused to attempt answers. Pre/post vocabulary and structured speaking tests were scored by Japanese-speaking researchers blinded to condition and timing. GELLI improved vocabulary recall and confidence relative to the videos, but the speaking difference was not significant. The bundled treatment cannot identify whether imagery, interaction, support or reflection caused the observed benefits.

Corrections and continuity remain fallible

Feedback exposed a further boundary: 110 of 264 instances were excluded because pronunciation and transcription errors could not be distinguished without retained audio. Seventeen of the remaining 154 were false alarms. Changing character appearances, inconsistent backgrounds and generation delays interrupted understanding; extensive help could overwhelm learners. Characters sometimes supplied information the learner intended to request, removing a practice opportunity. The single-session comparison had no delayed test, real-life transfer measure or equally interactive authored alternative. Missed goals, appropriate difficulty and reliable correction therefore remain concrete implementation problems. The study supports a promising practice format, not a claim that a richer display or generated content alone improves lasting language proficiency.
