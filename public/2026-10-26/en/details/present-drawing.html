The paper remains after the avatar disappears

Present Drawing joins the spectacle and keepsake of an autograph event: a visitor watches a virtual character draw while a real sheet acquires ink. The performer and observer need not occupy the same location, and a recorded performance can be replayed later. The paper organizes variations around timing, location, control source and avatar embodiment, illustrating five configurations. The interaction depends on seeing the trace emerge alongside the character’s movement, rather than merely receiving a printed picture afterward. Its evaluated case is narrower: one prerecorded virtual-performer autograph. Multi-site, live and system-controlled examples establish design possibilities, but their reception was not measured in the participant study.

Capture movement and strokes, then align their spaces

Authoring captures body motion and tablet strokes as one event package. During performance, a headset controller attached to the plotter provides a shared spatial anchor. A server sends avatar motion to the headset and path commands over a grblHAL serial connection. Two stepper motors move a carriage behind an acrylic drawing surface; magnetic coupling carries that motion to the visible marker mount. A third motor switches magnetic polarity to lift or lower the pen. The housing conceals rails and motors so visitors mainly see the paper and marker. This produces a persistent physical artifact, but requires the plotter, headset, server and calibrated installation rather than working on an arbitrary tabletop.

The avatar waits for the mechanism

The plotter cannot reproduce every rapid change in the original performance. The server therefore switches timelines: idle motion follows recorded timestamps, while drawing follows a timestamp estimated from the position reported by the mechanism. Keeping the avatar near the marker can temporarily place a live avatar behind the performer’s newest pose; it catches up after the stroke. Reported position can itself lag, so the arrangement is not evidence of precise closed-loop tracking. Participants noticed motor noise, nearly uniform speed and occasions when the hand appeared to catch the moving pen. Direct position sensing and time-aware control are proposed improvements, not capabilities already implemented or evaluated.

Eight observers, one prerecorded event

Eight institutional participants, excluding the authors’ laboratory, stood about one metre from the device and watched the same avatar produce two autographs. The prerecorded character did not respond to them. Comparisons against the midpoint of seven-point ratings supported attributing the drawing to the avatar and finding the output unremarkable as an autograph. Synchronization naturalness and an increase in presence during drawing did not significantly exceed neutral. There was no comparison with ordinary plotting, video or an in-person event, and no purchase-intent measure. Only one marker and A4 paper were tested; the mount favored a right-handed posture. Moving viewpoints, other media, noise and live personalization still need field evaluation.
