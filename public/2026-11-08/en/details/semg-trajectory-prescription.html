Muscle activity is the target; an exercise is the output

A participant holds a robot handle and applies force to move an on-screen ball toward a target. The model’s input is an expert-specified time series of desired muscle activity, not a general request to rehabilitate someone. Its output is the target trajectory used in that exercise. This makes the activity pattern a basis for discussing task design instead of considering only the shape drawn on screen. Deciding whether the target is appropriate remains a separate responsibility. The experiments use isometric force production. Movement of the displayed ball must therefore not be described as the robot moving a patient’s limb through an equivalent physical path.

Existing sensors form a calibratable loop

The setup combines a KUKA cobot, a custom handle, a Bota force sensor and Delsys surface-EMG equipment recording eight upper-limb muscles. The contribution is the mapping between desired activation and exercise trajectory, not a newly invented electrode. A transformer models the sequence, while flow matching supplies a distribution of possible trajectories and personal data can support fine-tuning. In a practical workflow, calibration includes electrode placement, signal checks, example collection and training time. Those steps compete with time spent exercising. If a changed placement requires substantial repetition, the fastest inference stage would not make the whole session fast. Setup burden needs to be considered alongside prediction accuracy.

Healthy-participant results establish feasibility

The authors evaluate fifteen tracking tasks from sixteen healthy participants and conduct a five-person live prescription test. This establishes a way to study the mapping on the experimental platform, not improved recovery for patients. Offline trajectory prediction and live activation error are also different outcomes and should not be collapsed into one accuracy figure. Individual strategies, fatigue and electrode placement can influence the mapping. Familiar and unfamiliar exercises consequently deserve separate attention when interpreting performance. The system is an engineering proof of concept for further validation. The paper does not establish a clinical service, a commercial price or a therapeutic benefit for a particular patient population.

Uncertainty should support a decision to defer

Flow matching favors trajectories near the training distribution, but the authors explicitly distinguish that tendency from a safety guarantee. High uncertainty could support collecting more calibration data before attempting a poorly represented target. It should not silently squeeze every requested activation pattern into familiar exercises. An actionable notice would explain what additional information is needed rather than showing an unexplained score. Compared with a fixed exercise library, personal prescription adds sensing and maintenance work while offering a finer adjustment mechanism. Its eventual usefulness depends on reducing trial-and-adjust effort under controlled conditions and preserving expert decisions about the target, load and when to stop. Those questions remain beyond the healthy-participant demonstration.
