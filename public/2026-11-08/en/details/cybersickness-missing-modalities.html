Breaking the Modality Barrier · Paper / research prototype

This VR discomfort model can work without an entire sensor stream, but real-time warning remains untested

2026-11-05T03:36:10Z · · Read 11-08 09:13Z

Different headsets expose different inputs

A VR experiment may collect gaze, head motion, cardiac activity and skin conductance, while another setup lacks an entire channel. A model that requires every input can turn that equipment difference into a deployment barrier. This paper treats availability as part of the input, combining separate encoders, aligned representations and masked fusion. The potential product benefit is continued analysis when a whole stream is absent. Yet producing a number is different from having enough evidence to adapt an experience. An interface using such a model would need to show which signals supported the current estimate, especially when a user changes devices or declines a particular form of sensing.

An availability mask is not data repair

The attention mask excludes an unavailable modality instead of fabricating a replacement stream. This handles whole-stream absence, not every form of damaged data or intermittent dropout. A modality never encountered during training is also outside the supported setup. Those distinctions have direct interface consequences. Permission withheld, hardware absent, connection lost and poor-quality measurements should not all appear as a numerical zero. A zero can look like evidence of comfort, whereas missingness means that evidence was not obtained. Keeping these states distinct would make troubleshooting and consent clearer. It would also let a product explain reduced confidence without implying that the person must enable additional sensors to continue using the experience.

Offline samples do not establish timely intervention

The authors evaluate three existing datasets with windowed processing and harmonized label ranges. Roughly half of the windows have zero discomfort labels, leaving more severe experiences relatively uncommon. Real-time system latency is not validated. These boundaries matter because a warning delivered after an unpleasant segment has ended may be accurate yet unhelpful. Mapping different rating scales onto a common numerical range likewise does not establish that the underlying subjective experiences are identical. Average prediction error is consequently only one piece of evidence. A product team would also need to understand errors during the less common severe episodes and the delay between a change in experience and a usable estimate.

An adaptive experience needs its own evidence

A prediction could inform a rest suggestion, a movement-speed adjustment or a choice to continue, but these are possible designs rather than validated outcomes of this paper. An automatic pause might interrupt a cooperative activity; repeated false alarms might make later prompts less credible. Preserving user control and making exit easy would therefore remain important even with better estimates. The modular input approach could make experiments across devices more practical, but each content setting and audience would still need examination. Its supported contribution concerns prediction with missing sensor streams. It does not show prevention of motion sickness, establish a diagnostic service or make the user’s own report of discomfort unnecessary.
