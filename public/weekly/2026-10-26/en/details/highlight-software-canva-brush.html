Generating an image and revising a design

The previous week’s Craft announcement concerned image generation. Brush addresses the next job: revising an existing design. Canva’s news and research indexes differ by one day, October 29 versus 30, but both place this addition inside the covered week. Seen together, the products suggest a practical sequence: choose the imagery, then work on text, scale, placement and consistency between pages. Someone preparing a poster or proposal often needs to revise one object after the first draft. Keeping editable material makes that possible; it does not decide which parts of the document the owner has already approved.

Keep the revision inside the editor

Initial Brush access covers supported edits for Pro subscribers. A person opens or attaches a design, selects the model and describes the revision. Canva says an editing loop executes code, inspects the page and leaves editable objects behind. This gives manual correction a practical entry point: select a heading or move a photograph rather than rebuild a flattened poster. Scope still needs to be explicit. A request to recolor every heading should not silently alter body text, data or chart labels. Seeing the resulting page is different from knowing which details the owner had previously signed off.

[S04]

Review a broad request in smaller pieces

The existing conversational editor recommends simpler layouts and smaller requests. Its help page describes stopping a run, undoing unexpected changes and identifying objects by position, size or color; layered documents and long conversations can also take longer. A practical revision can therefore begin with one page before expanding its scope, with a known original retained for comparison. Magic Edit serves a different task: mark an area within a photograph and generate a replacement. Choosing the right level of editing matters. A request about an editable heading should not accidentally become an image-replacement task just because both interfaces use conversational language.

Brand compliance still needs a separate check

The launch leaves Brand Kit support, reference attachments and image-heavy layouts as work in progress. Existing brand templates do not establish that Brush enforces their constraints. A useful comparison uses a familiar deck and asks three questions: were all intended objects changed, was unrelated content preserved, and how much manual repair remained? That is closer to delivery effort than generation speed alone. A fast restyle may still require rechecking every figure and brand element. Teams whose templates encode strict requirements have a reason to retain ordinary editing until the revision behavior is dependable on those specific documents.
