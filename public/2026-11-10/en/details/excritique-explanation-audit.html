ExCritique · Paper / interaction research prototype

ExCritique exposes the evidence behind explanation scores, including confident mistakes

Make the score itself inspectable

ExCritique addresses a specific evaluation problem: a visual-question-answering system can produce an explanation that sounds plausible but is unsupported by the image. The interface links distributions of several automated scores with a map of examples, allowing a reviewer to select a slice and inspect individual records. Its purpose is to help investigate explanations and the metrics judging them, rather than automatically certify a model. Our interpretation is that an evaluation interface should support both kinds of doubt. The answer can be wrong, and the model or rule assigning its quality score can also be wrong. Combining everything into one overall number can conceal both failure modes.

Keep distinct views of the same example

The three metrics examine visual support, discrimination between answer options and coverage of relevant visual concepts. The interface keeps the image, question, prediction, explanation and scoring evidence together. For a reviewer, this could reduce the bookkeeping involved in matching spreadsheet rows to separate images and logs. We would begin with disagreements between metrics, such as an explanation that mentions many relevant concepts but lacks visual support. Such cases can help distinguish a useful explanation from a persuasive justification assembled after the answer. Filtering only prioritizes attention, however. It does not create a reliable error label or determine whether the right remedy is data, prompting, scoring or model changes.

The timed task tests inspection behavior

The formative work involved nine AI researchers and practitioners. A subsequent study had sixteen participants use ExCritique and a spreadsheet-style baseline to identify explanation issues or questionable scores in ten-minute tasks. The interfaces exposed comparable information, with coordinated exploration as a key difference. The material came from two visual-question-answering datasets. Improvements reported for that inspection activity should therefore remain tied to the study setting. They are not improvements in the evaluated model’s predictive accuracy. Our additional concern would be adjudication: finding more suspected failures is useful, but a later assessment should check whether reviewers agree on which failures are real and what evidence establishes them.

Connect inspection to a reproducible development decision

For adoption, we would retain the example identifier, input version, model response and scoring configuration alongside each confirmed issue. Without those records, a later model update can change the result while leaving the same informal complaint, making regression difficult to assess. A small team inspecting only a handful of examples may still find a spreadsheet cheaper to prepare. Coordinated visualization is more compelling when reviewers repeatedly slice a large collection or investigate clusters of related errors. Commercial plans and deployment commitments were not established here, and we did not run its models. The useful next comparison is whether the interface improves a reproducible development decision, not merely how many points a reviewer opens.
