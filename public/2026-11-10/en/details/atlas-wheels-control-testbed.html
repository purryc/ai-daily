Wheels create a different load case

The new wheeled build is explicitly a research testbed. A custom adapter joins a scooter rim to each existing ankle actuator. Commands and reference movements feed learned whole-body control rather than a separately advertised warehouse vehicle. Rolling creates a useful engineering question: can a policy remain stable while its support points move under load? Our reading is that this is a test of the interface between a physical mechanism and its controller. It is not a purchase option or a measured improvement in warehouse throughput. No customer price or delivery commitment for the wheeled configuration is given, and a successful maneuver does not establish an entire shift of reliable work.

Model the physical constraints before training

The technical account says measured actuator behavior is used to calibrate simulation. That matters because an idealized motor can let a policy learn actions the physical machine cannot deliver. Earlier Atlas work explains the surrounding process: define a reference, shape rewards, train in simulation, test on hardware and iterate from observed behavior. It also describes varying load, friction and motor strength during training. Those earlier experiments are background, not new measurements for the wheeled build. A useful engineering review would ask which physical limits were modeled, which disturbances were sampled and which failures remain outside the tests. The current release does not supply a comparable reliability table answering all of those questions.

Separate the product from the experiment

The standard Atlas product page focuses on material handling and enterprise integration. Orbit is described as the connection to MES, WMS and other systems of record, with work and fleet performance available for oversight. Tactile sensing, surrounding cameras and autonomous battery swapping belong to that standard product description. They should not be silently transferred into a claim that the modified wheeled robot has completed the same application or safety integration. For a factory, the relevant output is a correctly handled part with a known task state. Choosing a platform still involves workstation assessment, training and exception handling. Better low-level control cannot by itself eliminate those adoption steps.

Look next at failures and recovery

Earlier technical documentation emphasizes repeated subassemblies and replaceable modules, making serviceability part of the platform design. Our assessment separates movement completion from recoverability: when a motor cannot follow a command or contact conditions change, an application needs a detectable state and a safe handoff. The wheeled account identifies actuator-failure response as ongoing work, not a general solution already demonstrated. A bounded follow-up test would repeat the same reference under documented conditions and record failures and operator interventions. That proposed test was not performed here. It would be more useful for assessing deployment readiness than counting only the successful movements chosen for an edited demonstration.
