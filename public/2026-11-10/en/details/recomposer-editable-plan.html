Inspect decisions before seeing the finished image

reComposer is a research interface for people who know the purpose of an image before they know its exact appearance. A planning agent turns the initial request into visible decisions such as subject, composition and palette. The user revises that plan before producing the final image. This is not an announced Apple consumer feature. For a brand illustration, our suggested starting point would be the requirements that cannot change: the character, the intended message and the elements that must appear. Showing those decisions before a polished rendering gives the creator a chance to reject an assumption before investing effort in small corrections around it.

Use words and spatial controls for different decisions

The prototype combines conversation with controls suited to particular visual decisions. A composition can be arranged spatially, while a palette can be adjusted directly or extracted from a reference. The final request combines the chosen elements and their influence settings. The contribution is an inspectable intermediate representation, not a claimed new image foundation model. Our adoption question is how clearly the interface communicates the scope of an edit. If a background revision also changes the person, or a palette revision disturbs typography, the creator needs to locate that dependency. A collection of controls is useful only when it makes such unintended changes easier to understand and correct.

Judge influence settings against the output

Influence settings distinguish an omitted element, a loose guide, a stronger preference and a locked requirement. This allows one decision to remain fixed while another stays exploratory. A product assessment should still compare the output with the requirement: expressing a lock to a generative model is different from enforcing geometry in a vector editor. For a storyboard or icon family, we would inspect the whole series rather than a single attractive example. A useful trial could vary one composition while keeping the subject unchanged, recording every correction needed afterward. If those corrections remain extensive, the planning interaction should be evaluated together with that downstream repair work.

Eight participants provide an interaction signal

The authors compared the interface with chat-based generation in a study involving eight professionals who make images for their work. Participants reported greater control and ownership. That is a useful interaction signal, but it does not establish broad productivity, commercial adoption or dependable output quality. Public service pricing and deployment regions were not established here. Our design takeaway is to preserve revisable decisions instead of making every conversational turn immediately produce a finished artifact. A subsequent evaluation should examine whether people with different experience understand those decisions, and whether the extra planning work is justified for ordinary tasks. Long-term correction effort matters as much as initial enthusiasm.
