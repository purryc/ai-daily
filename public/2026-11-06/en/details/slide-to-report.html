Slide to Report separates VR symptoms with physical sliders, making reporting training part of the interface

Leave a continuous symptom trace by hand

A questionnaire after a VR session can lose the timing of discomfort. This apparatus maps three symptom categories to physical sliders with corresponding feedback inside the scene. It provides a timeline for experience research rather than automatically interpreting a person’s bodily state. The interaction should be assessed when hands are occupied, when users cannot look down and when symptoms make continued reporting difficult. An unchanged slider is ambiguous: the person may feel unchanged, forget to update it or focus on finding an exit. Treating every stationary value as evidence of stable comfort would overstate what the input device can measure.

Simple hardware still requires learned categories

The hardware uses linear potentiometers and an Arduino, while physiological and gaze measurements are collected separately. More channels do not automatically teach people to distinguish nausea, disorientation and eye-related discomfort. Following a pilot, the authors extended training to ten minutes. An adoption comparison should therefore include explanation and practice, not only effort during the main task. Observe whether users consistently map a sensation to the same category or choose one merely because the apparatus asks for it. The convenience of continuous recording matters only if the distinctions remain meaningful to the people supplying the labels.

The comparison was not a generic overall rating

The main study involved 52 people in a between-subject roller-coaster experiment. Its methods define the single-slider condition as eye-related discomfort, while the three-slider condition records separate categories. Broader abstract wording should not replace that operational definition. The authors report relationships with corresponding questionnaire subscales and no statistically reliable burden difference. This does not establish superiority over every possible single overall scale, nor equivalence of effort. Physiological sensors shown in the apparatus are measurement equipment. Their presence should not be presented as evidence that a consumer product can already predict discomfort accurately in real time.

Keep stopping easier than reporting

For application research, first practice the categories in a mild setting, then compare sliders with verbal reporting and later questionnaires for omissions and interference. A tabletop arrangement may cease to be convenient in a mobile or intensely interactive task. Stopping should remain easier than continuing to report, rather than making a complete dataset the participant’s responsibility. The useful design outcome is locating a reproducible troublesome segment and testing an improvement. Continuous numbers should not be packaged as precise medical measurements. We did not independently replicate the study, and commercial deployment or cross-task reliability was not established.
