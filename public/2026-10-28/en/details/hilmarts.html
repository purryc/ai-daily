Moving the tablet moves the model

HILMARTS addresses a specific preparation step: aligning a three-dimensional model derived from preoperative imaging with the body in front of the operator. Holding a grab button makes the virtual model follow the tablet’s six-degree-of-freedom motion. The operator moves the device, releases the control and judges the approximate overlap, rather than manipulating only numerical rotation and translation controls. Automatic registration refines that initial pose. Human spatial judgment narrows the search, while geometric computation handles the finer adjustment. The output remains an aligned model in an augmented-reality view; the system does not independently choose incisions or perform surgery. Evaluation used a physical phantom, not patients.

Depth measurements need anatomical filtering

A Unity client on a LiDAR-equipped iPad Pro uses ARKit to capture synchronized color images, depth, camera parameters and pose. A local server handles the more expensive registration computation. BlazePose identifies the torso in the color image, and its mask is projected into the depth data to reduce irrelevant points from the table or surroundings. After removing outliers, the pipeline estimates candidate rigid transformations and refines them with point-to-plane iterative closest point registration. The relevant combination is an existing tablet sensor with operator feedback. It is not an entirely on-tablet computation, and segmenting the visible torso should not be confused with recognizing every internal anatomical structure.

Coverage feedback exposes incomplete geometry

During scanning, surface points are projected onto an occupancy grid. The largest connected empty region determines a coverage score, and registration waits until the score exceeds a seventy-percent threshold. This provides feedback tied to captured geometry instead of an undifferentiated waiting indicator. In the sixteen-person study, mean registration error fell from 12.82 mm after manual alignment to 3.31 mm after automatic refinement, with the overall procedure taking roughly four minutes. Four participants reset capture once. However, the difference between feedback and no-feedback conditions was not statistically significant, and their order was fixed. The gain from the complete pipeline cannot all be attributed to the coverage display.

A phantom does not reproduce moving tissue

The cohort contained fifteen STEM students and one clinician. Its usability ratings describe first-time interaction with a prototype, not prolonged clinical use or patient outcomes. The authors also note that supine imaging and lateral positioning can produce tissue deformation, while the current approach primarily estimates a rigid alignment. For product designers, the useful interaction is showing when captured data are sufficient and when more acquisition is needed, rather than presenting only a precise-looking overlay. A good coverage score still cannot guarantee correct registration. Clinical use would require evaluation of breathing, posture, occlusion and anatomical variation, along with understandable failure indications and a clear account of what the operator must verify.
