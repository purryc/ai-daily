Behavioral tasks retain their browser interface

CogArena implements ten behavioral paradigms as individual web applications, including recall, visual recognition, risky choices, repeated games and foraging decisions. An agent operates the page through its browser scaffold; jsPsych records stimuli, actions and timing, and the server scores the resulting trial data. Researchers can keep the visible task instead of rewriting an interactive experiment as a prose question. The public platform supplies tasks and scoring interfaces rather than requiring every submission to use one agent. For the paper’s four-model comparison, however, all models used Browser-Use. Its results describe that combination of model and scaffold, including how screenshots are presented and actions reach the page.

Three levels answer different questions

Level one checks whether the interaction produced valid data, sufficient trials and a genuinely finished experiment. Level two measures task performance, such as accuracy, reward and false alarms. Level three tests behavioral signatures established in human research. A preference and a capability cannot be interpreted as the same kind of achievement: declining to reproduce a human bias need not imply weaker ability. An unmeasurable signature must also be distinguished from evidence that a pattern is absent. Although the platform produces a composite score, the separate levels explain whether a session failed at page operation, task performance or behavioral resemblance. Those distinctions help determine whether to investigate the scaffold or the model.

A completion statement can precede the actual end

The authors ran four economy-tier multimodal models ten times on each task, giving 400 sessions. Only 167 passed every completion check. In 191 sessions the agent declared completion before the experiment actually ended. A random-key baseline finished all its sessions, which demonstrates reliable continuation rather than superior intelligence. Even among sessions passing the completion checks, only about 40% of testable behavioral signatures were reproduced. Repeated runs of the same model and task varied substantially. A single convincing trajectory consequently cannot establish stable completion or justify treating an agent as a human participant. The recorded trial sequence is more informative than the natural-language message announcing that the job is done.

The matching human comparison remains missing

The reported agent runs disabled per-trial response deadlines while retaining a thirty-minute and 500-step task budget. Configurable timing support therefore does not mean the evaluation imposed the same immediate pressure as a human experiment. More importantly, no human participants completed this version of the tasks. Human reference values come from original studies, so changes in implementation may contribute to observed differences. Dynamic stimuli are also limited by screenshot sampling. Product teams can borrow the separation of scoring levels and the explicit end-state check, but the work does not establish a general commercial bot detector. Nor does a high score demonstrate that an agent used the same cognitive process as a person.
