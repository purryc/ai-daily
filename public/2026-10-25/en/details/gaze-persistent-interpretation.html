When Interpretations Persist · Paper / research prototype

A gaze-aware assistant can carry an old misinterpretation into its next alert

Separating a visible event from an inferred intention

An assistant seeing somebody look at a knife and tomato can retain the visible objects or write that the person is preparing to cut. The second version reads smoothly but adds an intention. Once stored, that inference can become an apparent fact behind a later alert. This paper studies the problem using gaze-aware video tasks, without introducing a new eye-tracking sensor. Four earlier recognition tasks supply 4,513 historical entries for six later tasks, including object transitions, scene recall, gaze sequences, future actions and proactive alerts. The question is how retained meaning changes the next output, rather than whether the original description sounds fluent. A natural sentence can hide an important difference in evidential status.

Changing the representation while holding retrieval fixed

The experiment freezes direct source answers and retrieves only earlier events from the same video. Retrieval uses independent event and query representations, selects three similar entries and presents them chronologically. Generated interpretations do not participate in retrieval or see future frames. The comparison then retains only answers, writes free-form interpretations, or separates Observed, Inferred, Uncertain and Temporal Scope fields. Qwen3.5-9B is the primary model, with GLM-4.6V-Flash checking the core pattern. The fields are not an automatic correction system: an incorrect recognition can still enter memory. Holding events, order and target input fixed makes the contribution of representation inspectable without establishing the reliability of the complete memory pipeline.

Reducing misleading influence has a tradeoff

Among 648 diagnostic cases with three incorrect source answers, free-form interpretation raises the rate of originally correct later answers becoming wrong from 7.3% to 13.7%; structured evidence fields reduce it to 6.0%. There is also a cost among 714 cases with three correct source answers: helpful changes fall from 50.6% with free-form interpretation to 47.9% with structured fields. These homogeneous groups are diagnostic slices. Mixed histories account for 61% of eligible retrievals and are excluded from this comparison, though included in ordinary task accuracy. Across six tasks, mean accuracy is 58.2% for structured fields, 57.1% for free-form history and 52.0% without history. Individual tasks, including scene recall, still favor no history.

Correcting a record differs from deleting it

A separate intervention changes the most relevant historical entry in 117 already harmful cases. Correction using source-time ground truth recovers 41.03%, deletion recovers 25.64%, and a similar-length neutral replacement recovers 11.11%. Because the correction can use experimental ground truth, it is not a product’s automatic repair rate. Two author annotators also audit content; recognizable field structure prevents complete blinding. For a continuously observing assistant, the findings support carrying time and inference status with a memory while deciding separately whether a later task needs it. They do not establish wearer comfort, trust or long-term use. More retrieved history is not uniformly better assistance, even when its representation is carefully structured.
