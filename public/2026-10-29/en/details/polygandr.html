Making “more like this” actionable

Choosing an image often precedes finding words for it. PolyGandr lets a person supply a base image and contrasting examples, then vary the strength of the change. Consider an exploratory task such as finding a character expression for a packaging series. A designer could compare overall impressions, retain promising candidates and only then articulate the useful differences. This may reduce the effort of translating intent into a prompt, but it does not remove selection work. If the references differ in color, shape and composition simultaneously, the resulting direction may combine several changes. Each output still needs to be checked against the actual brief rather than accepted simply because it resembles a reference.

A map for finding neighbors

The prototype uses UMAP for the global map and adjusts the base latent vector using the difference between contrasting examples. The linked map and local mixing panel offer two scales of exploration. As an interaction principle, consistency between those scales matters: a person inspecting a nearby variant should be able to relate it to the broader search already undertaken. The map is useful to think of as a worktable for arranging and comparing candidates. It should not be treated as evidence that nearby points differ in exactly one attribute. Compressing a complex generative space into a plane inevitably leaves some distinctions difficult to express. A practical interface should therefore keep the base, references and actual output visible together instead of asking the user to trust a coordinate alone.

Separate resemblance from correctness

The paper explores images and kirigami structures, but a shared interaction does not imply shared acceptance criteria. A mood board may succeed once it communicates a useful direction; a physical object still needs dimensional, connection and material checks. That distinction changes what a team should preserve with an output. References, selection rationale and the relevant constraint version may be as useful to the next person as the image itself. An adoption trial could begin with early concept work where discarded variants are inexpensive. For a precise numerical change, direct parameter editing may be easier to explain. Forcing every requirement through latent-space movement could increase review work instead of reducing it.

Can the chosen option be recovered?

A useful practical observation is whether a designer can recover a previously preferred result and explain why a subsequent adjustment changed it. Split a trial into open exploration and constrained revision, then examine retaining candidates, returning to earlier outcomes and handing work to another person. Raw generation speed would miss those costs. The paper’s domain-expert walkthrough does not establish everyday efficiency for all designers or commercial availability. Product teams also need to ask what happens to a saved direction when the underlying model changes. Until a concrete compatibility guarantee exists, the sensible operating assumption is that exploration state belongs to a particular model and working session.
