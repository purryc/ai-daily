Fit the cameras to the machine

A fixed depth-camera housing can dictate the shape of a robot head and the spacing of its eyes. OAK 4 FFC separates the modules from their shared computer, letting a builder choose viewpoints before packaging the electronics. A forward stereo pair can coexist with side and rear views. That arrangement reduces the need to put a separate processor at every viewpoint, but it leaves the mechanical layout to the builder. Cables, mounts and clear windows still need space. Extra views also do not automatically produce extra depth coverage: a camera can reveal an obstacle without providing a stereo distance measurement. The useful design choice is which directions need depth and which only need visual awareness.

Calibration needs overlapping views

Factory calibration describes each lens; it does not know where that lens ends up on a new machine. The builder supplies approximate positions and orientations, then overlapping cameras observe textured surroundings so their alignment can be refined. Views without overlap retain the entered geometry. This distinction helps locate faults: a broken seam or unreliable depth map may come from installation geometry rather than object recognition. Recalibration after a mount moves is useful, but a repeatable mount remains necessary. The advertised pipeline includes synchronized capture and onboard processing. A surrounding view is not automatically a complete surrounding depth map, and a collection of images is not already a fused three-dimensional reconstruction of the robot’s environment.

Compute, sensing and outputs have different jobs

The datasheet lists a six-core processor, Linux, five MIPI camera interfaces and motion sensors. Images support visual inference, while gyroscope, accelerometer and magnetic measurements can feed a machine’s motion software. Their presence does not amount to a validated navigation system. A simpler machine may run application logic on the board; a more demanding robot can treat it as a perception subsystem that supplies depth, detections or tracks to a central computer. The choice depends on the complete workload, not a headline compute figure. The specification also makes simultaneous streams and processing combinations conditional on modules, firmware and pipeline design. Lighting, camera spacing and calibration remain part of the depth measurement, even when inference happens locally.

Ordering and delivery have separate dates

The October 27 announcement opens orders and lists the developer kit at $849, with cameras purchased separately. It schedules kit shipments for November and barebone deliveries for early 2027. The current product page says the kit ships now, but that undated state does not establish a shipment before this issue’s cutoff. The datasheet also labels the configurations prototypes and allows for final hardware changes. A kit includes cooling and development connections; a barebone integration leaves more thermal, power and enclosure work to its owner. For a lab, the kit is a practical way to begin arranging and testing cameras. For production planning, board revision, module compatibility and actual supply commitments still matter independently of an order button.
