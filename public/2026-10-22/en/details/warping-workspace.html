Visibility is only the beginning

A teleoperated humanoid can have reachable objects beside or behind it while its operator sees only a narrow forward view. Repeated head turns, body rotation or camera switching then interrupt manipulation. Warping the Workspace sends monocular 360-degree imagery from a RICOH THETA X to an Apple Vision Pro and changes the corresponding upper-limb control mapping on a Unitree G1. The contribution is not simply fitting more pixels on screen: the displayed direction differs from the physical wrist direction, requiring an inverse mapping before inverse-kinematics control. This is research on visual–motor correspondence for assisted teleoperation, not autonomous humanoid task execution.

Three different tradeoffs

The fixed condition preserves the original directional mapping. Global Zoom compresses 360 horizontal degrees into 180 with a yaw gain of two. Focus+Context keeps that coverage while magnifying the central plus-or-minus 15-degree region by 1.5 and compressing the periphery. Eight participants manipulated the fixed-base robot’s upper body to place bags from front, side and rear positions into a 40-centimeter frame, producing 72 trials. Trials stopped after five knockovers or five minutes. This supplies explicit task and failure boundaries, but limited repetitions per participant, condition and direction do not represent a busy warehouse, mobile base or prolonged deployment.

Completion and preference differ

Fixed view succeeded in 20 of 24 trials, Global Zoom in 23 and Focus+Context in 21; rear-target successes were four, eight and five respectively. Global Zoom improved rear reachability in this experiment, yet six participants preferred Focus+Context, with one preferring each alternative. Preference counts do not establish higher accuracy, and 23 successful trials do not establish production reliability. The prototype does not demonstrate advantages for sustained fatigue, variable network delay or recovery after tracking loss. Monocular panoramic video is not complete depth sensing either. Any reduced need to turn must be assessed alongside the effort of understanding the altered geometry.

Help the operator regain correspondence

Alternatives include wide-angle monitoring, multiple camera panels and ordinary imagery with manual camera or robot turning. They avoid remapping the wrist but add gaze shifts or operational steps. A pilot should hold targets and payload constant while measuring completion time, collisions, mistaken approaches, repositioning and manual recovery, extending to continuous tasks and brief video interruptions. Operators also need to recognize the current mapping and verify that view and action correspond after reconnection; these are our acceptance requirements, not verified features. If broader visibility demands more arm corrections, the interface has transferred search effort into control effort rather than reducing the total job. Practical evaluation should retain both objective completion and operator preference, since the reported divergence shows why a single aggregate score would hide an important interaction tradeoff for future interface design.
