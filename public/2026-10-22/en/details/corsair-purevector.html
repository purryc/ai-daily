A new input mapping

CORSAIR and Aimlabs introduce PureVector on the SABRE v3 PRO family in an October 20 announcement, placing it in this issue’s three-day supplement. The mechanism personalizes sensitivity across movement directions for players who repeatedly tune aiming settings. The earlier approach changes a uniform DPI or sensitivity value and relies on trial and error. This workflow measures directional behavior before storing an individual mapping. It is appropriately described as adaptive input calibration. The public material does not establish a model architecture that would justify inventing a claim of live AI aiming during gameplay.

The complete workflow

The official guide begins with mouse and receiver firmware updates, selection of the usual DPI in Web Hub and an Aimlabs account. Setup questions precede five one-minute calibration rounds; saving writes the result into the mouse firmware. Gameplay then needs no background application. The interaction change is portability of the personalized mapping rather than running an assistant again on every computer. That convenience still depends on correct initial configuration. If the entered sensitivity differs from the game’s actual setting, a user needs a clear way to notice that the comparison no longer uses equivalent input conditions.

Feedback and removal

The calibrated interface offers refinement and removal and presents directional results. Its After comparison requires additional tasks instead of appearing immediately. That waiting state matters because it separates an applied setting from a measured outcome. Only one DPI stage is calibrated; other stages do not automatically receive the profile, and changing the calibrated stage requires removing the existing data. Our interpretation is that clearly indicating whether the mapping is active matters more than adding charts. Otherwise a user can mistake an uncalibrated setting for an optimized one and draw the wrong conclusion about the device.

Metrics and purchase boundaries

The vendor’s up-to 81 ms and 33-percent claims concern the aiming-and-correction phase, not total reaction-to-shot time or win rate. Task scores and elapsed time measure different outcomes, and we have not reproduced transfer across games. The launch says the series is available October 20, while the US MG product listing observed here showsUS$199.99 and a November 17–20 shipping window. A family launch is not immediate delivery of every configuration. Shape, mass and price also need comparison separately from calibration. A software benefit cannot be assumed to compensate for a physical form that does not suit an individual user.

Adoption judgment

A falsifiable comparison should preserve the original settings, alternate calibration on and off under comparable tasks, and add game situations not used for calibration. Record time, overshoot, fatigue and recalibration effort. Manual DPI tuning, in-game sensitivity and continued practice remain alternatives. Gains confined to a familiar training task would weaken the adoption case, as would frequent setup work after a grip change. Fewer corrections in actual use, combined with understandable activation and removal, would make calibration a more dependable input tool. A vendor’s patent reference does not establish either independently verified performance or patent scope examined in this research.
