An earable delivers a private cue, but a missing head tilt is ambiguous

A head movement becomes the reply

The prototype connects an earbud’s audio output to motion-based input. A person can hear a cue without reaching for a phone and reply while remaining in the shared activity. The screen character is operated by a researcher, so the study concerns the communication arrangement rather than autonomous perception and reasoning. Our design interpretation is that a reply should be tied to a clearly bounded prompt. Otherwise, ordinary movement can acquire a meaning the wearer never intended. A production interface would need to distinguish being available to answer from having already answered, and make that distinction perceptible without interrupting the surrounding conversation.

No movement is not necessarily a negative answer

A three-second detection window maps absence of a qualifying tilt to a negative response. That is a convenient experimental rule, but an assistant needs a richer interpretation. A wearer might miss the audio, need more time, decline participation or perform a movement the sensor misses. We would therefore treat timeout as unresolved in consequential applications and offer a brief retry or correction. Recognition accuracy and correct interpretation of intent should be separate measures. Neither a detected motion nor a missing one should silently acquire broader authority than the prompt expressed. Repeated prompts also need a stopping rule so that recovery does not become pressure to respond.

[S123]

Protocol completion and acceptance differ

Forty people participated in paired gameplay, with and without the channel; non-wearers learned about it afterwards. Completing the prescribed exchange does not establish that everyone accepted the arrangement. Interviews included both teamwork and unfairness. A product can protect message content while still explaining what role the private channel plays. A family reminder, a collaborative exercise and a competitive game distribute benefits differently. Evaluation should therefore ask affected non-users as well as wearers. A favorable usability score from the person receiving useful information cannot stand in for a judgment from somebody whose choices or outcomes are altered by that information.

Make response recovery part of the interaction

This is research, not an announced retail earbud feature. Prolonged comfort and everyday use remain unestablished. A useful follow-up would include movement during listening, intentionally ignored prompts and correction after a wrong response. Alongside completion time, record accidental triggers, timeout behavior and what bystanders understood. The design opportunity is a compact channel that supports both receiving and responding. Making it dependable requires visible or audible state, a way to retract an answer and a clear boundary around the action being requested. These are proposed evaluation steps, not reported findings, and should be tested in the intended setting before the technique is generalized.
