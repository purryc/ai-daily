PHIL previews a robot’s next motion so operators can take over before an error

Preview motion before deciding to intervene

Reactive correction starts after a robot has already deviated. PHIL places predicted end-effector motion in the physical workspace, letting an operator hold a trigger to intervene and release it to return control. The interaction provides an earlier decision window rather than a guarantee that the preview is correct. An adoption study should check spatial alignment, occlusion and whether operators distinguish predicted from executed movement. Delayed or ambiguous traces could increase takeover effort despite displaying more information. This distinction matters especially when a person must watch the object, the tool and the overlay at once, rather than inspect a stationary visualization at leisure.

Retain predictions that were not executed

During takeover, the policy continues producing shadow predictions on a fixed schedule without commanding the arm. A rejected prediction is paired with the subsequent executed continuation from the same query state and horizon; the accepted prediction at release is excluded from negative samples. This alignment avoids comparing unrelated situations, but continued takeover remains an implicit preference signal. A training tool should let people inspect the resulting pairs and distinguish intentional corrections from pauses, calibration problems or unfinished decisions. Such review is an adoption recommendation, not a feature independently tested here. More correction data is not automatically better if its labels capture operator hesitation rather than a meaningful behavioral preference.

Evaluation rollouts are not participant counts

Each condition uses five correction episodes and twenty-five evaluation rollouts; an episode may contain several interventions. Diffusion Policy covers four tasks and multiple demonstration budgets, while X-VLA covers three tasks. The reported average improvement over HG-DAgger changes both correction collection and the training objective, so it cannot be attributed only to the headset preview; separate ablations investigate those factors. The recovery analysis examines successful episodes. For an operational comparison, retain failed runs, timeouts and interventions as well. Otherwise a smoother-motion statistic may exclude the very runs that determine whether the system is useful to a person responsible for completing the work.

Measure intervention cost before expanding

This research fits teams that already have robot hardware and a policy-training workflow; buying a headset alone does not provide a general robot service. Compare it with screen-based teleoperation, stopping and restarting, or collecting additional demonstrations on a recoverable task. Measure detection timing, takeover duration, repeated transfers of control and subsequent full-task performance. If a specialist must watch traces continuously, a higher policy score may still fail to reduce total training cost. Public materials do not establish long-term novice usability, commercial pricing or a complete licensing audit. Those gaps should remain separate from the demonstrated feasibility of collecting proactive corrections.
