Lilo · Paper / interaction research prototype

Lilo lets children rehearse difficult group chats with contextual mentor interventions

Practice a response without sending it to classmates

Lilo places a child inside a simulated disagreement about a birthday invitation that excludes one character. The child writes a response, two artificial peers react, and a mentor offers contextual guidance. The output is an exchange to practice within, rather than a polished answer to copy into a real chat. Its concrete purpose is to make experimentation less consequential than messaging classmates. Our design interpretation is that the interface should keep the simulation explicit while leaving the child responsible for the response. If success merely means discovering the wording the system rewards, the exercise could teach compliance with a chatbot rather than judgment about another person.

Separate the characters from the intervention decision

The implementation separates three character agents, an orchestrator and an end-of-session feedback agent. Role-specific prompting uses Claude 3.7 Sonnet without fine-tuning. The orchestrator chooses speakers, intervention timing and difficulty. A safety layer can withhold a flagged message from the peer agents and show a red indication. Our interaction question concerns the meaning of that signal: a message that was not sent differs from one that was allowed but prompted reflection. Children should be able to distinguish those outcomes. We would inspect false flags on ordinary disagreement, because removing every uncomfortable exchange could undermine the very negotiation and perspective-taking that the exercise is intended to support.

The outcome is short-term self-report

The exploratory study involved 28 children aged nine to eleven in a single session. Interaction was in Hebrew; the paper’s interface examples show an English-localized build. Participants reported greater confidence afterward, but there was no control group or follow-up of actual group-chat behavior. Spoken questionnaires and the fixed scenario leave alternative explanations. A fluent example is therefore not evidence of lasting educational benefit, nor is a feeling of safety a complete safety evaluation. Our proposed next step is a different conflict without immediate coaching, observing how children explain their response choices. Repeating the same exercise would be a weaker test of transfer.

Keep rehearsal separate from live assistance

The authors distinguish rehearsal from an assistant intervening in a real dispute. We consider that a meaningful product boundary: an incorrect suggestion in live communication can immediately affect another person. A future service should make exiting, reviewing the exchange and seeking a trusted adult straightforward. It should not substitute the agent’s interpretation for family or educator judgment. We did not verify a family-facing download, subscription price or regional release. The November 10 afternoon submission falls inside this issue’s window. The event reported here is the research record and its observations, not a claim that the intervention works for every child or prevents conflict.
