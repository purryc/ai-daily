MKA evaluates cross-file visual work, but a rubric score is not whole-task completion

Visual files enter; working artifacts come out

MKA is a research benchmark from Google and Artificial Analysis, not a new office assistant. Two hundred private tasks span ten sectors and 83 occupations, with ten separate public examples. Inputs combine visually dense files, and agents must produce usable artifacts. Google describes a weighting of 75% factual accuracy and 25% visual design. Our interpretation is that this makes page inspection a meaningful part of evaluation rather than an optional embellishment to text extraction. Broad occupational coverage still does not establish representative sampling of every profession. Teams should ask which tasks resemble their own work before translating a benchmark score into a staffing or automation decision.

Public examples expose the format, not the entire private suite

A workforce-planning example in the report combines flow charts, headcount data, rules and a workbook template to produce departmental rankings. The visual relationship feeds a calculation rather than merely decorating the prompt. The Hugging Face card describes metadata, input and rubric files. We read that card and the report’s example description, but the individual JSON pages did not open in this tool; no task was run. For a team-specific trial, we would first identify the relationships that require looking at an image, then trace their effect on the output. Accessible examples should not be confused with independently reproducing the private two-hundred-task leaderboard.

Shared tools and budgets make execution comparable

Artificial Analysis runs models through Stirrup in a Linux sandbox with code, search and image-viewing tools and a 250-turn ceiling. A separate judging agent inspects deliverables against individual criteria. The headline Climb WSR measures weighted satisfaction of harder criteria, not the fraction of entirely successful jobs. Our product judgment is to examine failed requirements, editability and the human work still needed. Similar scores can conceal different consequences: minor layout defects and an incorrect dimension extracted from a drawing are not equivalent operational failures. A purchasing or integration decision therefore needs an error breakdown tied to the intended task, rather than a ranking alone.

Agreement and ranking have defined limits

The report’s 96.2% human–autorater agreement comes from fifty tasks emphasizing higher-weight criteria; it cannot establish equal reliability on every formatting detail. Each evaluated model ran once per task, so the intervals omit repeat-run variability. The top three reported Climb scores were statistically indistinguishable, and the live leaderboard can add models. We therefore avoid treating a current ordinal rank as a durable conclusion. Our proposed next step is repeated runs of the same relevant tasks, examining failures before comparing cost per acceptable output.
