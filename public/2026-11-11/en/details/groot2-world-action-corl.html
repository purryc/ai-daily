Predict a consequence alongside an action

GR00T 2 was introduced at CoRL on November 9 and is included here as a recent supplement. It reads recent video, an instruction and robot state, then jointly predicts future video and an action chunk before executing and observing again. Robot-specific heads translate the plan for different bodies. The research page explicitly distinguishes observed history from generated future frames. That distinction matters when reading a demonstration: a plausible predicted image is not proof that the robot completed the physical action. Our assessment would keep prediction quality and execution quality as separate questions, even when both are displayed together in an attractive video comparison.

Keep the denominator attached to few-shot results

The authors report 40% average zero-shot trial success across 50 tasks, with at least one success on 40 of those tasks. One demonstration per task raises average success to 71%; five raise it to 95%. Those denominators answer different questions and should not be substituted for one another. For a team choosing a system, the relevant comparison includes the objects, placements and tolerances in its own task. Correctly choosing the sequence can still fail at a precise contact. The reported results are useful evidence of adaptation under the authors’ protocol, but they do not establish reliability for an arbitrary household chore or factory station.

Make recovery part of an observable workflow

The accompanying announcement describes an agent using the GR00T 2 API to sequence skills and recover from errors. The research account separates task-level reasoning from precision manipulation. For product design, that offers an opportunity to expose why work paused and what recovery is being attempted. It does not make recovery automatically safe: a mistaken diagnosis can cause repeated physical attempts at the wrong step. A useful engineering review would preserve failure conditions, intervention points and the outcome of each recovery. It should compare complete tasks with and without the agent, rather than adding up successful grasps and calling that an end-to-end success rate. We have not run that comparison.

Separate training tools from model delivery

Isaac Lab 3.0 is introduced alongside the model, with physics options and reinforcement post-training support; Newton addresses simulation involving cables, cloth and articulated hands. Training-side capability should not be mistaken for solved real-world deployment. The GR00T 2 research page currently says code will be available in December. Calling the work open therefore does not establish that every artifact needed for reproduction is downloadable today. A practical evaluation still needs weights, licensing, embodiment support and interface versions. Our proposed starting point is a bounded manipulation task with a clear pass condition, followed by a measurement of adaptation effort and recovery behavior. Buying hardware on an assumption of general competence would skip those unresolved steps.
