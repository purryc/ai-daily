Project Progress Desk ties agent receipts to checklists and separates evidence from estimates

Identify the project that needs a decision

Project Progress Desk is a local Windows research interface that groups projects across agent sessions. A person scans blockers and decisions, opens the relevant project, and returns to its linked conversation to continue. The task is recovering context after time away, rather than improving code generation itself. Our interpretation is that the overview should help choose the next action before it celebrates completion. A nearly finished project waiting on one important decision may require more attention than a less complete project that can run independently. A useful trial would measure whether users find that decision quickly, not merely whether they can read a progress bar.

Connect receipts to a shared acceptance definition

Agents submit outcomes, supporting files, blockers and next steps. The Desk relates those receipts to a weighted acceptance checklist and updates the overview. Different fills distinguish receipt-supported progress from retained estimates. The demonstration uses simulated project data, which should remain visible in its caption. Our product question begins with the checklist: who chooses the weights, what counts as a pass, and how missing evidence appears. Each progress change should lead back to a requirement and its supporting artifact. An agent declaring completion should not silently replace an unverified criterion or alter the denominator. The numerical display is a representation of chosen requirements, not an independent measure of project quality.

Keep unsuccessful approaches available

The detail view preserves alternative approaches and their recorded outcomes. The paper describes a hardware project where component simulation checks did not imply that integration, configuration and quality testing were finished. That distinction is useful when a local success risks being repeated as a whole-project completion claim. Our proposed adoption test would ask a new collaborator to explain why an earlier approach was abandoned and whether its evidence still applies. Keeping only the successful summary makes repeated dead ends more likely. A useful history must retain the conditions of the result as well as the result, so that old evidence is not treated as current by default.

Daily use is not a controlled comparison

The authors report lab use beginning in September, involving about thirty members, and describe a 36-project workspace. A broader deployment and controlled comparison against a conventional project table remain future work. This does not establish a general time-saving figure. The paper says source release is planned after acceptance; public download, pricing and service regions were not verified. Its November 9 submission is treated as a recent research supplement. Our remaining concern is upkeep: maintaining receipts and revising requirements can itself become work. A realistic evaluation should count that effort alongside any reduction in time spent reconstructing context from conversations.
