A direction still needs a destination

A person who cannot continuously operate a conventional joystick may still be able to express left or right. Those directions do not fully specify which table to approach. MLNS investigates that gap using a modified powered wheelchair. In the experiment, participants pressed directional buttons on a game controller, with at least three seconds between commands to emulate filtering used in some limited interfaces. Certain conditions reversed a command with a probability of twenty percent. Participants travelled from a fixed starting point toward two desks or a person and stopped when they considered themselves to have arrived. Brain signals, head controls and disabled participants were not tested here. The October 28 timestamp records the paper submission, rather than establishing its first public demonstration.

Visual targets and footprint checks

The platform carries two forward Hokuyo lidar sensors, a RealSense D435i camera above the user and wheel encoders. OpenNav combines YOLO-World with MobileSAM to locate objects; the experiment configured it to seek either a desk or a person. A priority queue ranks user commands first, visual targets second and a default point ahead last. Model predictive control supplies a route, whose sampled points guide a reactive avoidance layer. That layer considers the four corners of the wheelchair footprint instead of treating the chair as one central point. This matters when a route clears the centre but leaves insufficient room for the body. Direct reverse commands are inhibited because the sensors cannot establish that the space immediately behind is clear.

What the 360 runs showed

Ten nondisabled participants attempted thirty-six runs each: three targets, three conditions and four repetitions per combination. The conditions were unperturbed input, perturbed input without visual targeting, and perturbed input with visual targeting. The authors report 359 completions and one participant-requested interruption, with no collisions across the attempts. For target X, visual guidance averaged 91.5 seconds and 3.1 commands, compared with 108.4 seconds and 5.3 commands under perturbed input without visual guidance. Target Y went the other way: 77.3 seconds and 5.8 commands with vision, versus 74.0 seconds and 4.7 commands without it. The overall completion rate therefore cannot be attributed solely to the visual component, and the data do not support a claim that every route became faster.

Similar objects still require a choice

Target Y lay beyond other tables along a corridor. The system could favour a nearer reachable table, leaving the person to issue more corrections; precise final alignment also remained a human task. This makes target choice an important interface concern alongside object recognition. For a future everyday mobility system, the selected destination and the effect of an override would need to be understandable to the rider. Otherwise, sparse steering could be mistaken for agreement with the system’s choice. The paper establishes a modified research platform and controlled trials, not a production feature, clinical benefit or an all-day safety guarantee. The authors identify larger samples and more heterogeneous environments as future work.
