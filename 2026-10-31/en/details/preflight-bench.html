A correct plan can click the wrong place

A computer-use agent can select the correct button in a screenshot and still click the wrong control moments later. A banner may arrive after planning and push the form downward. The paper illustrates this with author-captured airline screenshots: a fifty-two-pixel shift moves a loyalty-points option under a previously planned search click. Preflight inserts a check immediately before the action. It asks whether the target remains in place and whether a dialog obstructs it, then either proceeds or requests a new plan. That checks consistency between the screen and an existing intention; it does not prove that the intention matches the user’s goal. The October 29 submission describes independent author research, without endorsement from the vendors compared.

Position checks and outcome checks differ

The checker retains two planning-time screenshots separated by two hundred milliseconds and masks pixels that already change, such as blinking cursors and loading animations. It then searches near the planned position for the target image patch and tests for obstruction or a dialog. The reported pixel check takes 312 milliseconds including the sampling interval. A failed check requests replanning rather than shifting the old coordinate to a similar-looking control. A different check asks whether an action actually succeeded, which requires interpreting what the screen reports. The study evaluates small vision-language models and OCR for that semantic question. Position validity and outcome confirmation therefore need different evidence, with different latency and false-alarm costs.

Public pages and synthetic stress tests

On thirty-one public pages permitting read-only automation, the author tracked visible links and buttons, with roughly 1,300 targets per state. Stale targets affected 0.4 percent of planned clicks within thirty seconds after loading, versus seventeen percent within the first second after opening. Six deliberately changing synthetic page types supplied 4,608 click pairs and 3,600 outcome pairs for stress testing. On held-out tests, the pixel check detected eighty-two percent of stale targets with four percent false alarms; Opus achieved eighty-three and twelve percent respectively. A 4B model detected all failures without false alarms in the outcome set, which the author acknowledges may be relatively easy. These are classification results under the stated conditions, not general agent task-success rates.

Waiting does not guarantee a stable page

A separate analysis uses October HTTP Archive records for desktop home pages. Only fifteen percent of sixty-five buying or booking pages became network-quiet within three seconds of HTML parsing. This measures loading and layout shifts, not observed checkout misclicks. The same gap did not persist on mobile, and authenticated or cart pages were not covered. The author reimplemented ten agents’ waiting rules rather than running those agents end to end. In a synthetic live loop, pixel checks reduced stale clicks but increased step time by twenty-eight to 105 percent under the preregistered accounting of 6.5 seconds per replan, missing the overhead target. Savings appeared under measured planning time instead. Adoption therefore needs workload-specific timing and error costs, rather than extrapolating a faster individual check into whole-agent savings.
