A motion clip does not specify muscle activation

A skeleton raising an arm does not tell an animator how strongly the biceps should contract or how its volume should bulge. Artists may create separate activation curves, then revise them when the motion changes. RLMuscle takes reference motion and musculoskeletal assets, learns an activation trajectory that drives the movement, and uses it to produce volumetric deformation. It does not scan a performer in real time or require a new wearable sensor. For character teams producing close views of human motion, the proposed saving is part of the work of guessing activation muscle by muscle. Geometry, correspondence between assets and the final shot still need production preparation and judgment.

Solving control in a simpler model

Full volumetric muscles have many deformation degrees of freedom, making them expensive and difficult to place inside a reinforcement-learning loop. The authors instead train a policy and muscle network together on a one-dimensional Hill-model surrogate. This stage produces both skeletal motion and activation. Retargeting and muscle mapping then pass those outputs to the volumetric solver. The control asset has 284 one-dimensional muscles, while the displayed asset has 194 volumetric muscles; these describe different representations and should not be combined. Both stages use the same Hill-type force law, linking activation to deformation rather than adding an unrelated bulging effect after skeletal animation. The detailed simulation can then run as a forward pass.

Handling contraction, volume and bone contact together

In the volumetric stage, activation becomes contraction along the fiber direction, while volume constraints produce bulging across it. Repeatedly correcting only a fine mesh can converge slowly and leave inverted elements at high stiffness. Geometric multigrid passes corrections through a coarser tetrahedral cage before returning to the detailed mesh. Collision handling keeps muscle tissue from penetrating bone. The project page separately shows fiber-constraint, multigrid and collision comparisons, making their different visual responsibilities inspectable. These are animation-solver mechanisms, not measurements of every muscle in a particular person. Nor should a swimming demonstration be read as evidence of a complete fluid simulation. The intended output remains a controllable character asset and animation.

What the 18-millisecond result includes

The project’s timing covers fifty frames of full-body volumetric simulation on an RTX 4090 with collision, multigrid and fiber processing enabled. It reports 18 ms per frame without export and 32 ms with USD export. Approximately 98 MiB of peak memory excludes another roughly 386 MiB of runtime context, so the smaller number is not a complete project budget. Training and final rendering are outside that timing. The paper also identifies weaker multigrid behavior on slender muscles and leaves volumetric simulation inside the training loop for future work. Integration begins with asset and retargeting compatibility. Solver timing alone does not establish how much manual curve editing or shot-delivery time a production team would save.
