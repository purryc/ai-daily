Finishing a run does not finish the user's work

Atlas focuses on the moment a researcher returns to an agent's results. Papers, hypotheses, experiments, and summaries may exist without making clear how they address the original question. The workspace links three levels: the goal, the decisions, and the artifacts. A persistent goal anchors a trajectory tree showing directions pursued, deferred, or ruled out, with outputs connected to the decisions that produced them. This problem is relevant to personal research tools, but the study recruited researchers rather than general consumers. The product change is not a longer-running agent. It is an interface that lets a person understand consequential choices without rereading the conversation from its beginning or treating the final answer as the entire investigation.

Ask different questions of the same material

The other half of the interface provides alternative artifact projections. The same paper set can be clustered by topic, placed on a timeline, compared in a table, or represented through relationships to hypotheses and experiments. Users move from artifacts back to decisions and from decisions to their outputs. The authors distinguish changing representation from tracing provenance: one changes how a collection is examined, while the other explains its origin. The product value is avoiding a separate manual matrix each time the user's question changes, with context lost during reconstruction. This still depends on recorded links and rationales. Attractive branches cannot recover decisions the agent never recorded, and a displayed explanation is not necessarily the only explanation of its behavior.

The baseline still contained the information

Fourteen researchers completed a within-subject comparison with counterbalanced interfaces and topics. The baseline included chat, an artifact viewer, and an agent that could summarize on request. Its execution log contained the same pursued, rejected, and deferred directions. Atlas added the unified canvas, trajectory tree, and coordinated projections. Each block included training followed by a twenty-minute, six-part review task, which should not be confused with sustained workplace use. Across twenty-eight prepared runs, there were an average of 170.4 artifacts and 15.9 trajectory turns; agent execution averaged 83.9 minutes. This demonstrates a substantive output-volume problem while making the comparison clearer: Atlas was not tested against a baseline stripped of history or summary capability.

Greater legibility is not greater correctness

Median decision-transparency ratings were 6.0 for Atlas and 3.8 for the baseline; every participant preferred Atlas for understanding decisions, and thirteen favored it on the overall preference item. Participants reported lower workload and clearer next steps, while eight treated deferred or rejected branches as possibilities to revisit. The compelling finding concerns legibility rather than quantity: identical information could feel effectively invisible inside a linear log. Outcomes largely rely on subjective scales and interviews, not measured scientific accuracy. Some participants wanted to continue directly from a branch, which is a request rather than established functionality. My editorial judgment is that post-run inspection deserves a first-class place in agent products, while retaining links to original evidence so structural clarity does not become unwarranted certainty.
