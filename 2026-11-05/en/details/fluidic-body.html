Steering direction and formation together

A target point alone does not specify how a swarm should behave. Passing a narrow gap calls for deformation; pushing an object calls for cohesion. Fluidic Body maps hand shape and movement to a virtual fluid region that guides the robots. Opening or closing the hand selects soft or hard behavior. The interface therefore gives the operator a material-like control alongside direction. A useful design question is whether people can anticipate what the selected formation will do before it reaches an object. Predictable transitions matter more than an attractive resting shape when the task mixes movement and contact.

Each sensor has a defined job

The physical apparatus uses 30-millimeter two-wheeled maru robots. Photodiodes decode projected patterns for localization; an Intel RealSense D435 tracks objects, while a Leap Motion Controller 2 tracks the hand. The host sends positions every 100 milliseconds. These components explain why the interaction is more than a gesture-recognition demo. A deployment decision would need to include installation, tracking coverage and the work surface, rather than pricing only the robots. Removing a wearable does not remove environmental infrastructure. A tabletop demonstration also provides no evidence that the same interaction is ready for a room, uneven floor or airborne swarm.

Separate controlled comparison from demonstration

The controlled VR comparison involved 17 participants. On the combined avoidance-and-transport task, all completed with the fluid controller, compared with eight using the RVO baseline. The physical evidence comes from SIGGRAPH 2025: 88 self-selected visitors provided responses without a baseline or fixed trial protocol. The samples should not be combined into one controlled experiment. A team considering adoption should ask which outcome it needs: task completion, perceived control, precision or bodily ownership. Improvement on one does not settle the others, and a conference visitor’s short experience cannot establish performance after repeated everyday use.

Precision remains constrained by lag

Latency was a frequent complaint in the physical demonstration, where radio updates and turning before forward motion affect responsiveness. Only two discrete flexibility settings were evaluated. Continuous control and larger or three-dimensional swarms remain open questions. A practical follow-up could record unwanted contact, stranded robots and corrective movements during the same mixed task. It should also check whether the operator can recognize lost tracking without consulting another display. Fewer commands would be useful only if waiting and recovery do not consume the saved effort. This paper supports further interface experimentation, not a purchase recommendation for a finished household assistant.
