Give an unseen current a tactile cue

A forward camera can show a target without explaining why a vehicle drifts away from its path. This study communicates cross-currents through vibration and target distance through changing softness, while the operator steers and controls speed on the same pad. The mechanism places environmental feedback where the hand is already engaged. A design evaluation should still examine interference between pressing to accelerate and interpreting the tactile signal. Combining input and output on one surface saves a visual channel, but it does not automatically make the mapping effortless or prevent an operator from confusing a control action with a warning.

Separate the feedback and sensing mechanisms

Particle jamming changes softness, while load cells sense position and pressing force. Participants operated a BlueROV2-Heavy toward a buoy across a controlled cross-current at fixed depth. This is a physical in-water interaction study rather than a simulated tactile animation. Its controlled conditions are also material to interpretation. A visible target and predictable hazard do not reproduce low visibility, changing depth or an unexpected obstacle. Before applying the interface to a working operation, a team would need to establish which environmental signals are available and how an unavailable or stale signal is communicated to the operator.

Faster operation is not the same as lower workload

Eight participants experienced still water, a cross-current without haptics, and a cross-current with haptics, then repeated trials after familiarization. Haptics coincided with higher speed than the current-only condition, alongside the highest mental-demand and frustration ratings. Speed was used as a proxy for confidence, not a direct measure of appropriate risk judgment. The later phase still involved the same novices after brief practice. It should not be presented as a separate expert cohort. For product design, the mixed result is useful: more informative feedback may improve action while making the person more aware of how demanding the situation is.

Test the cost of interpreting the signal

The current paper adds cognitive and behavioral analysis to previously reported positioning and path results. It does not announce a new commercial device. A follow-up could measure direction-recognition time, incorrect turns, approach speed and recovery when cues conflict. Faster movement would be an ambiguous outcome if the interface encouraged confidence without helping people notice a misread signal. Professional operators, dynamic three-dimensional disturbances and degraded visibility remain important tests. Comparing these cases with an additional visual instrument would help establish when tactile feedback is worth its learning burden, rather than assuming that any extra sensory channel improves the task.
