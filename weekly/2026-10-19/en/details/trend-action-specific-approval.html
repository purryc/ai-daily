Three controls with different scopes

The October 22 1Password announcement connects agent sign-in to a decision on the owner’s device. Its product documentation explicitly leaves subsequent account behavior to the agent and website. Monish appeared on Product Hunt on October 23 with a lock-screen channel for answering agent questions. Kimi Code 2.3.2, released October 25, makes swarm execution depend on a mode the user enables. These separate teams address credential use, remote response and work delegation. Together they support a limited product-supply proposition: a broad request for help is being broken into identifiable operational decisions. They do not establish interchangeable protection across those layers.

Approval existed; its placement is changing

There is an earlier comparison. Meta’s September 8 Muse announcement already described asking before sending email or making a purchase. A readable community discussion subsequently proposed tests involving a changed recipient, revoked account access and uncertain outcomes; those proposals were not executed security tests. This week therefore does not introduce approval from scratch. It continues a design question about where the decision belongs and exposes gaps between account access, action permission and confirmation of the result. The available archive lacks repeated behavioral measurements for the same products over several weeks, so neither adoption growth nor reduced interruption can be reported.

Fewer prompts can hide an unresolved boundary

The mechanisms themselves supply counterevidence. Monish relies on its caller to honor the response; a notification surface cannot enforce the caller’s stop behavior. Kimi changes ordinary delegation instructions and separately checks swarm execution, which are different strengths of control. Preference Is Not Permission finds that personal policies reduce model overreach without removing it in role-play scenarios. An alternative explanation is that these releases are individual usability repairs rather than convergence on a shared control design. Confidence is moderate in the direction of product design, while actual risk reduction and willingness to pay remain unestablished.

Change the conditions after approval

A small comparison can use synthetic orders or test messages: keep the task fixed, then change only the destination account, recipient or amount. Record what the person saw, the scope allowed and the final write result, without connecting live payments or customer data. Also expire a request, revoke permission and simulate an uncertain network response. The proposition strengthens if the product rejects stale approvals without requiring every unrelated step to be reconfirmed. It weakens if the only persistent state is a green approved label, or if successful login is reported as successful task completion. This is a proposed evaluation, not work performed for this issue.
